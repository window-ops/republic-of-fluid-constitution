The economic order

Why the Republic is socialist

Why the Republic holds its workplaces, land and knowledge in common, what that does and does not commit it to, and what the people who disagree say about it.

Article 6 abolishes income drawn from the bare holding of a productive asset, and Article 6(2) holds the means of production as commons, cooperative usufruct, communal enterprise and public establishments. This section explains why, records what the Republic's opponents say about it, and lists what remains unresolved.

The short version

No one draws an income from owning a thing that produces. Workplaces are held by the people who work in them, land and water and knowledge by everyone, and health and education by public establishments their users help govern. The state owns almost none of it, which is the difference between socialisation and nationalisation, and the reason this Republic is not what the twentieth century meant by a communist state.

The Republic has run this arrangement since 1890 and has got it badly wrong twice: once between 1954 and 1974, when a planning directorate closed its workings and set targets in tonnage, and once in the winter of 2024, when a constitution abolished currency without saying what should allocate anything instead. Both failures are in the record, and both shaped the present text.

Three parties in the chamber want the economic order changed, and Article 53(4) protects them in saying so. Their arguments are set out here in their own words, alongside the strongest arguments made against planned economies generally, and the answers the Constitution actually gives.
