# Quirks

The small things a visitor notices in the first week. Transport, technology, connectivity, and the social habits that come with them.

## Roads and trucks

Traffic keeps right and everything is metric. Vehicle dimensions, masses and axle loads are the same everywhere in the country, and the test station at Vestrano signs off on all of it.

The length limit is measured over the whole combination, trailer included, which is why every truck on the road has a flat-fronted cab with the engine underneath. Anyone hoping to import a long-nosed American truck eats two metres of payload and the combination goes over the limit. The few that exist in the country are museum pieces and one recovery truck at Sornorum that moves under a permit.

The motorway network is small. One route runs north to south from Lindoma through Sornorum to the port at Lutoliluri, with a western spur toward Durorilaqui, and that is the whole of it. Motorways stop outside the towns they serve. Nothing wide and fast passes through a built-up area.

Most of the traffic goes through the sub-regional network: two-lane roads, maintained by the sub-region, connecting villages to their market town. Long-distance freight goes by rail.

Streets inside the towns are narrow. Widening one means a spatial plan, a public inquiry and a habitat connectivity test, and proposals to widen have a poor record at all three stages.

## Exceptional Transports

An oversized or overweight load is an Exceptional Transport, the same idea as a French convoi exceptionnel and with recognisably similar signage.

Anything over the general limits on length, width, height, mass or axle load needs a permit, and permits are public documents. Each one names its route, its window of movement, its escort and the date it expires.

| Stage | What happens |
| - | - |
| Survey | Bridges, culverts and overhead lines on the route are checked before the permit issues, and the survey is published with it |
| Escort | Escort vehicles carry the standard lights and boards, and their crews direct traffic under the authority of the communal police |
| Timing | Movement through a town happens at night or outside working hours, with the communes told in advance |
| Local conditions | A commune can add a stricter condition for safety or accessibility, provided it blocks nobody else's lawful journey |
| Afterwards | Damage to a road, a bridge or a watercourse gets repaired at the mover's own cost |

Two categories run outside the ordinary process. Reactor components and turbine assemblies move under a multi-year corridor authorisation reviewed once a year. Rescue and recovery vehicles hold a permit that lets them skip the survey in an emergency and file it afterwards.

## Cars, pods and buses

Private cars are rare. Cars sit above the resource intensity threshold, so communes, federations and public establishments hold them and residents book them by rotation or by lot. Nobody is allocated a car of their own, and no amount of the residual unit will buy one.

Fleets are distributed evenly, so a rural commune keeps more vehicles per resident than a city does. Lindoma has fewer cars than anywhere else in the country.

Lindoma has also run a network of small automated pods since 2019, four to eight seats, on segregated lanes between the neighbourhood centres and the tram lines. They are booked through the public information system and supervised by identified controllers at all times. The experiment gets reviewed annually and no other town has adopted it yet.

Public transport is free at the point of use and there are no fare gates or ticket machines anywhere. Rail is the spine, with the 1907 Lindoma to Lutoliluri line still the main artery, the northern coastal line to Leriduson, and the western line through Sederna to Durorilaqui. Coaches run by the sub-regions cover the places where trains cannot reach. Where a network crosses a boundary, the councils on both sides run it jointly.

## Technology

The sector looks strange from outside and follows from a handful of rules: patents are prohibited, knowledge made with public resources belongs to everyone, and devices are furnished as provision.

Every person who needs one gets a computer and a phone, along with a network connection. Phones are thick, heavy, open down to the last component, with a battery that comes out without special tools, a replaceable screen, standard fasteners and published schematics. Parts stay free for at least ten years and there is a repair workshop within reach of every commune.

The Republic maintains no operating system and prescribes none. Systems come from communities and cooperatives, and the person holding the device holds administrative control over it. Most phones run Plasma Mobile or Phosh, Android is a minority, and iOS is absent because nobody can import one and nobody would get it licensed.

Devices carrying software the user cannot examine, replace or remove are prohibited outright, along with remote disablement and anything that only installs from one source. That rule quietly excludes most of the world's consumer electronics, which is the main reason imports are a small and slightly ceremonial business.

Imports arrive through the translation cooperatives, which buy lawfully abroad for their members. The Fairphone 6 release produced a rare queue, followed by an evening of reflashing for every single purchaser, since the mobile core runs on free software and proprietary stacks do not work here. Guides for the process are pinned in every tool library.

The Harbour Road Repair Cooperative in Lindoma imports current foreign handsets each season purely so the public can try to open them. Fewer contestants manage the battery every year without breaking something and the display challenge is worse. The angry legal letters this generates get published in the cooperative's register.

## Dial-up in the countryside

Broadband reaches most of the country. In the villages, a large number of households still connect using dial-up.

This has nothing to do with poverty. The copper is already in the ground, and digging up a lane to lay fibre or run DSL means using disruptive machinery, so village assemblies vote the works down with some regularity. Proposals for a mast fare worse, since somebody always objects to a crane behind their house.

Almost all of the modems in the republic run past the 56K ceiling, being domestic designs squeezing everything available out of a voiceband line. Bonding two or three lines with multi-link PPP on top of that gets a hamlet to a speed that handles the register, the news, mail and a video call without complaint. Mountain lines still drop for days at a time.

## Social norms

**Nobody asks what anyone does for a living.** Provision is the same for everyone, so the question carries no information about status and lands oddly. People ask what someone is working on, which is a different question.

**Everyone has served on something.** Bodies drawn by lot are common enough that most adults have sat on a restorative panel, a deliberative body or an oversight college at some point. An enterprise cannot refuse to release someone who has been drawn, and turning down a place carries no stigma either.

**Care counts as work.** Looking after a child, a parent or a neighbour is labour, is counted in the plan, and reduces what someone is expected to contribute elsewhere. Saying "I'm at home with my father this year" describes a job.

**The week is short.** Twenty-four hours over four days at most, so weekday afternoons are busy in a way visitors find disorienting. Libraries, workshops, allotments and swimming pools fill up quickly.

**Gifts, yes. Selling on, no.** Handing something to a friend is normal and expected. Selling something received in provision is void and mildly disgraceful, so the second-hand economy runs on giving things away, and most neighbourhoods have a shelf or a table for the purpose.

**Repair before replacement.** Tool and instrument libraries are everywhere and heavily used. Buying a new thing because the old one broke invites the question of whether anyone looked at it first.

**Officials get no honorifics.** There is no president, no prime minister and no minister, and committee chairs are addressed by name. People criticise the plan freely and in public.

**Sixteen votes.** Political rights start at sixteen, including for people in detention, so school-age political argument is taken seriously and campaigning turns up outside schools.

**Documents matter less than presence.** Being in the country is what confers rights, so nobody asks a stranger about status.

**Phones are ugly and this is a point of pride.** Nobody hides that their handset is a brick.

**Crime is a foreign topic.** Petty crime fell through the 2010s far enough that a teenager in Lindoma has no reference point for it. The old crime video games faded out partly for that reason, and partly because the newer ones need an account and a live connection to a foreign server.

**Strangers get talked to.** Conversation with whoever happens to be nearby is normal, and people generally like being noticed. Some people follow autonomist thinking and hold that nobody should be spoken to without their agreement while part of the youngest generation challenges their perceptions.

## A few more rules to know

Every product carries a public record of its materials, origin, energy of production, expected life and route of recovery. Building something to fail early, obstructing repair or withholding parts, tools or documentation is prohibited.

Construction, safety, accessibility, electrical, sanitary and food standards are set nationally and published free of charge in a form any person can implement and verify. Seismic construction standards apply everywhere, the peninsula shaking often and damaging rarely. Communes may go stricter on ecology, safety or accessibility.

Water is administered by watershed, and the watershed boundaries beat the administrative map. Abstraction is capped per basin and no dwelling is ever disconnected.

Animals kept for food range outdoors in social groups. Confinement systems that prevent that are prohibited.

The Republic has no official language. Anyone may deal with public authority in a language they understand, with interpretation free of charge, and Ossira runs the largest interpretation service in the country.
