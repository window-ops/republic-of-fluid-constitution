History of the Republic of Fluid

Three constitutions in one hundred and thirty-six years. The first was won by revolution and governed for one hundred and thirty-four years. The second was written in a hurry to end a deadlock, and broke within two years on problems its drafters had not thought about. The third exists because of what the second could not do.

The Republic of Fluid occupies the northern peninsula between Iosmein and Bor Brashavenko. Its recorded political history begins with the Kingdom of Fluid, established in the 1760s, and is conventionally divided into four periods: the Kingdom to 1890, the First Republic under the constitution of that year, the Directorate from 1954 to 1974, and the reform and opening that followed. The means of production have been socially owned continuously since 1890, which makes it the longest-surviving socialist constitutional order in the region.

Three constitutions have been in force. The first, adopted on 15 December 1890 after the revolution that ended the monarchy, ran to eleven articles and governed for one hundred and thirty-four years. The second, adopted on 1 March 2024 to resolve a four-year parliamentary deadlock, contained seven articles and lasted two years. The third, approved by referendum on 7 June 2026 and in force since 7 July, runs to three hundred and forty.

The Republic has fought no war since its foundation and has never been occupied, though it was blockaded in both world wars and subjected to a Western embargo from 1949. Its two gravest internal crises, the Directorate years and the failures of 2024 and 2025, both arose from defects in its own planning and constitutional arrangements, with no external pressure bearing on either. Each produced a body of written procedure intended to prevent its recurrence, and most of the present Constitution can be read as a response to one or the other.

The territory is poor in coal and has no oil. Uranium found in 1956 during the embargo gave the Republic an electricity system based on fission by 1970, an export trade from 1985, and a dependence that Article 32(6) now classes as transitional.

1Kingdom of Fluid, 1760s-1890

1.1Origins and society

The Kingdom was established on the peninsula in the 1760s. The economy was agrarian, with ironworks at Ferrista from 1763 and small workshops in the river valleys. Remoteness and a poor road network kept the merchant class thin, a circumstance to which historians of the Republic generally attribute the weakness of liberal politics in the nineteenth century and the corresponding strength of the workers' associations.

News of the French revolution reached Lindoma by ship in 1788. A reading circle of teachers, clerks and shipwrights met in the port for eleven years before the Crown banned it in 1799.

1.2The March Rising of 1848

In March 1848 peasants from Durorilaqui and shipyard workers from Lindoma held the capital for nineteen days. The Crown retook it with mercenaries hired from Bor Brashavenko and executed two hundred and six people. The employment of foreign troops against the capital fixed a suspicion of the southern frontier that outlived the monarchy by a century, and is still cited in debates over the closed border at Sarnova and Dromera.

1.3Industrialisation and the formation of parties

Thin coal at Vandorum and Kelvaqui and the first mechanised looms at Calmena drew population into the towns from the 1860s. The seams were shallow and poor by the standards of the period and were largely exhausted within two generations, which limited the scale of industrialisation and left the country without a domestic fuel base.

Four parties formed between 1881 and 1889: the Agrarians in the western plain, the Unionists in the ports, the Scientific Party among the teachers and engineers of Sederna, and the Youth Party in the schools. They agreed on little except the abolition of the monarchy.

2The First Republic, 1890-1954

2.1Revolution and the constitution of 1890

A general strike called by the four parties in March 1890 closed the ports, the railways and then the mines. In November the Lindoma garrison refused an order to fire, and on 15 December the King signed an abdication. The First National Council adopted the first Constitution of the Republic on the same day.

The text ran to eleven articles and was almost entirely procedural. It established the Council, placed the means of production in the hands of those who worked them, and left nearly everything else to be settled in practice. Its brevity is usually treated as the reason for both its longevity and its eventual failure.

2.2The Hungry Springs, 1891-1894

Land was transferred from the great estates to village committees faster than the committees could be trained to manage it. Two harvests failed and roughly nine thousand people died, most within eighty kilometres of Durorilaqui. The Council slowed the transfers and dispatched agronomists in place of commissioners.

The episode established by convention a rule that was not written into law for a further hundred and thirty-five years: that those who perform work are consulted before it is reorganised, and that local knowledge counts as evidence. It appears in the present Constitution as Article 25(1)(d).

2.3Parliamentary consolidation, 1895-1914

A parliamentary system was established in 1895. The four parties governed in changing combinations for fifty-five years. Schools and clinics were built through the 1900s and the Lindoma-Lutoliluri railway was completed in 1907. Literacy passed ninety per cent in 1912.

2.4The First World War and the first blockade

The Republic did not enter the war. A blockade closed the northern sea lanes for twenty-six months, and bread, coal and medicine were rationed by the commune councils. This was the first sustained experience of allocating scarcity by procedure, price having no part in it, and the first occasion on which a queue was treated as a failure of policy.

2.5Realignment and the Depression

The Unionists and the Scientific Party merged into the Humanist Socialist Party during the 1920s; the Agrarians and the Youth Party became the Democratic Socialists. Environment and Justice split from the Agrarians over the clearance of the western uplands.

The Depression reached the Republic through collapsed export orders. The Council answered with public works, declining austerity, and paid for the necessary imports by cutting the Vandorum forests. Roughly a fifth of the old-growth stand was gone by 1935 and the consequent erosion is still under repair, the replanting having begun in 1983 on a hundred-year schedule.

2.6The Second World War

Bor Brashavenko aligned with the Axis in 1939 and closed the land frontier, which has not reopened. A second and harsher blockade brought six years of rationing. Nineteen thousand refugees arrived by sea at Leriduson from the occupied eastern coast, and the decision to admit all of them was taken by the National Council in a single sitting against the advice of its own security committee.

3The Directorate, 1954-1974

3.1Embargo, uranium and the nuclear programme

Western governments imposed an embargo in 1949. Iosmein declined to enforce it and Reformed Luces Aporia quietly broke it, so the Republic retained its machine tools, but it had no oil, no coal worth extracting and no friendly supplier of either.

Prospecting for a substitute found uranium in 1956 in the uplands above Ferrista and beneath the Kelvaqui moor, together with monazite sands at Ravelluri yielding thorium and rare earths. The deposits were shallow and of high grade, and remain the only mineral wealth the country has possessed in quantity. A research reactor at Ferrista went critical in 1958 and the first power station entered service above Cortius in 1964. By 1970 fission supplied more than half the Republic's electricity.

3.2Planning under secrecy

The Central Planning Directorate was created in 1954 to manage the embargo economy and was intended to be temporary. Planning was professionalised and then closed to inspection on the ground that publication would assist the embargo. Targets were set in tonnage and transmitted downward; workplace assemblies continued to meet and ceased to decide.

The recorded consequences are specific. Goods were built heavy because mass met the target. Repair workshops closed in 1961 because repair generated no tonnage. The Marnaqui river was biologically dead below the Tavrino chemical works by 1966.

3.3The Cortius Report and the strike wave

An internal audit commissioned by the Directorate itself, known as the Cortius Report, was completed in 1968 and suppressed. The print workers who set it printed it regardless. The Directorate survived six further years, but the report ended any claim that its conduct was unknown.

In 1971 dockers at Lutoliluri struck over a loading target they held to be unsafe. Three were killed when a crane was worked past its rated limit. The Lindoma shipyards came out eleven days later, then Durorilaqui, then the country. The strike wave continued intermittently for two years. Its demands were procedural throughout: publish the plan, restore decision to the assemblies, count hours honestly, shorten the week. Reactor workers at Ferrista joined in 1972 and added the publication of plant safety records, which had never been shown to those who worked inside them.

4Reform and opening, 1974-2018

4.1The Assembly Reform

The Directorate was dissolved between 1974 and 1976. Planning was rebuilt as rounds of negotiation between workplace, communal and regional councils, with inputs and variants published. The working week was reduced to thirty-two hours. Most of Title IV of the present Constitution derives from this reform. Little of it comes from the revolution of 1890.

4.2Ecological accounting and repair

Environment and Justice entered government for the first time in 1978, and ecological accounts in tonnes and hectares were published for eleven industries. The Vandorum replanting began in 1983. Remediation of the Marnaqui began in 1977 and the first fish returned in 1994.

4.3Frontiers, the Bloc and the export trade

The Corvaluri crossing to Iosmein reopened in 1989 after ninety-nine years. The Democratic Socialist Bloc was founded at Lindoma in 1994 by Fluid, Iosmein and Reformed Luces Aporia.

Export of uranium concentrate began in 1985 and later extended to fuel assemblies and specialised components; every licence has had a safeguards condition, nuclear weapons being prohibited. From 2003 foreign economic entities were licensed to conduct the shipping, certification, insurance and brokerage that the Republic's own institutions could not perform in foreign jurisdictions. The renewable programme began in earnest in 1998 with the wind resource above Corcina, and public administration moved to free and open software from 2001.

4.4Climate impacts

Coastal retreat at Ravelluri removed roughly nine hundred metres of shore from 2013. The Quintorum basin failed three harvests in seven years. The Sornorum floods of 2017 displaced four thousand people from a floodplain that successive spatial plans had never closed to building.

5The Deadlock, 2019-2023

The governing coalition broke in 2019 over the pace of the fossil phase-out, the Humanist Socialists seeking to protect the Vandorum and Kelvaqui workforces and Environment and Justice seeking acceleration. No combination could assemble a majority.

The constitution of 1890 provided no procedure for dissolving the Council or for the failure to form a government. The Council sat for four years, unable to act and unable to be dismissed, while the Presidium governed for forty-seven months under an emergency power drafted for floods. A challenge to the third renewal was dismissed in 2022 in a judgment of one page holding that the Supreme Court had no jurisdiction.

In 2023 nine hundred residents drawn by lot, convened by the communal councils without legal authority to do so, sat for five weeks as the Lindoma Assembly and demanded a new constitution. A referendum in October authorised drafting with seventy-one per cent in favour.

6The second Constitution and its failure, 2024-2025

The second Constitution was adopted on 1 March 2024, drafted in eight months by a committee of the Council. It contained seven articles: a rights catalogue, cooperative ownership, the abolition of currency, and two safeguards inserted in the belief that the deadlock had been engineered from outside. It established no executive branch.

Its three failures are conventionally numbered. The first came in September 2024, when the Electoral Commission classified two parties under Article 2 and the State of Democratic Safeguard suspended ordinary legislation. One was the National Traditionalists at four per cent; the other a housing campaign polling at nineteen. Two hundred thousand people demonstrated in Lindoma within a fortnight and the Safeguard was lifted in November.

The second was the winter of 2024. Currency had been abolished and no allocation procedure replaced it. Insulation, antibiotics and spectacle lenses ran short in particular places with no lawful means of ranking claims, and allocation proceeded informally through acquaintance and position.

The third came on 10 June 2025, when turnout of 57.3 per cent fell below the sixty per cent that Article 2 made a condition of validity. The election was void and the text provided for nothing further. For fourteen weeks the Republic had no parliament and no lawful means of obtaining one; water, power, food, clinics and schools continued under the authority of communal councils and branch federations alone.

7The third Constitution, 2025-2026

A referendum on 21 September 2025 convened a Constitutional Convention of two hundred members and authorised an interim National Council of one hundred to sit until a new text took effect. Both were elected the same day, the turnout condition disapplied, on a turnout of 68.4 per cent.

The Convention sat in public from November 2025 to February 2026. Deliberative bodies drawn by lot examined each title and published their objections. Reasoning systems built to the Convention's specification and held in common were used to verify cross-references across the draft, model resource consequences and identify contradictions; Article 339 records that every choice they informed was made and minuted by a person, and that their use confers no interpretive authority.

The Convention adopted the draft on 12 April 2026 by 148 votes to 52. A referendum on 7 June approved it with 64.1 per cent of votes cast, no turnout condition applying. It commenced on 7 July 2026, repealing and preserving the constitutions of 2024 and 1890.

8Chronology

The Kingdom

1760s
The Kingdom of Fluid is established on the peninsula. The economy is agrarian, with small ironworks in the western uplands. Remoteness and a poor road network keep the merchant class thin, which will matter a great deal later.
1788
News of events in France reaches Lindoma by ship. A reading circle of teachers, clerks and shipwrights begins meeting in the port. The Crown tolerates it for eleven years, then bans it.
1848
The March Rising. Peasants from Durorilaqui and shipyard workers from Lindoma take the capital for nineteen days. The Crown retakes it with mercenaries hired from Bor Brashavenko. Two hundred and six people are executed, and the memory of the foreign troops fixes a suspicion of the southern border that outlives the monarchy.
1860s-70s
Thin coal at Vandorum and Kelvaqui and the first mechanised looms in Tessilius pull people into the towns. The seams are shallow and poor and are exhausted early, which is one reason the Republic will later go looking underground for something else. A working class forms faster than a bourgeoisie does. The propertied are too weak to capture the state, and the propertyless have ceased to serve one.
1881-89
Four parties form: the Agrarians in the arable west, the Unionists in the ports, the Scientific Party among teachers and engineers, and the Youth Party in the schools. They quarrel constantly and agree on one thing, which is that the monarchy has to go.

The First Constitution

1890
The four parties call a general strike in March. The ports stop, then the railways, then the mines. In November the garrison at Lindoma refuses an order to fire. On 15 December the King signs an abdication and the First National Council adopts the first Constitution of the Republic: eleven articles, mostly about who decides what.
1891-94
The Hungry Springs. Land is taken from the great estates and handed to village committees faster than the committees can be trained to run it, and two harvests fail. Roughly nine thousand people die. The Council slows the transfers, replaces the commissioners with agronomists, and afterwards writes into practice a rule it never wrote into law: consult the people who do the work before you reorganise it.
1895
A parliamentary system is established. The four parties govern in coalition, and will do so, in changing combinations, for fifty-five years.
1900s
Schools, clinics and the first rail link between Lindoma and Lutoliluri. Literacy passes ninety per cent in 1912.
1914-18
Fluid does not enter the war, and does not escape it. A blockade closes the northern sea lanes for twenty-six months. Bread, coal and medicine are rationed by the commune councils, which is the Republic's first serious experience of allocating scarcity, and the first time queues are treated as a policy failure.
1920s
The Unionists and the Scientific Party merge into the Humanist Socialist Party. The Agrarians and the Youth Party become the Democratic Socialists. Environment and Justice splits from the Agrarians over the clearance of the western uplands.
1929-35
The Depression reaches Fluid through collapsed export orders. The Council answers with public works, declining austerity, and pays for the imports by felling the Vandorum forests for timber. Employment holds. Roughly a fifth of the old-growth stand is gone by 1935, and the erosion that follows is still being repaired.
1939-45
The kingdom of Bor Brashavenko aligns with the Axis and closes the land border, which has never reopened. A second blockade, harsher than the first. Rationing runs for six years. Nineteen thousand refugees arrive by sea from the eastern coast, and the decision to admit all of them is taken by the Council in a single sitting, over the objection of its own security committee.
1947
The National Traditionalists form, arguing for restoration. They never win more than four per cent, and are never banned.

The Directorate

1949-53
Western governments impose an embargo. Iosmein declines to enforce it and Reformed Luces Aporia quietly breaks it. Fluid keeps its machine tools, and learns that its neighbours matter more than its principles are comfortable with. It has no oil, almost no coal worth the digging, and no friendly supplier of either, which makes fuel the central problem of the next decade.
1956
Prospecting for anything that might replace the imported coal and oil finds uranium in the western uplands above Ferrista, and again under the Kelvaqui moor. The deposits are shallow, extensive and of unusually good grade, and the monazite sands at Ravelluri yield thorium and rare earths besides. It is the only mineral wealth the Republic has ever had in quantity.
1958
The research reactor at Ferrista goes critical. The Directorate treats the programme as a question of survival, keeps it out of policy debate, and publishes nothing about it for sixteen years.
1964
The first power station enters service on the eastern seaboard above Cortius. By 1970 fission has more than half the electricity of the Republic, which is what allows a state with no oil and no usable coal to refuse the terms of an embargo outright.
1954
The Central Planning Directorate is created to manage the embargo economy. It is meant to be temporary.
1955-70
The Directorate years. Planning is professionalised, then closed. Targets are set in tonnage in Lindoma and sent down. Workplace assemblies keep meeting and stop deciding. Output rises and quality falls: goods are built heavy because heavy hits the target, repair shops close because repair produces no tonnage, and the Marnaqui river is dead below the chemical works by 1966. The Directorate publishes nothing, on the grounds that the embargo makes publication dangerous.
1968
The Cortius Report, an internal audit of the Directorate, is suppressed. A copy is printed anyway by the print workers who set it.
1971
The dockers at Lutoliluri strike over a target they say cannot be met safely. Three are killed when a crane is worked past its limit. Within eleven days the shipyards at Lindoma are out, then Durorilaqui, then everything.
1971-73
The Strike Wave. Two years of it, on and off. The demands are consistent and procedural: publish the plan, let the assemblies decide, count the hours honestly, cut the week. Markets and personal rule go unmentioned throughout.
1972
Reactor workers at Ferrista join the strike wave, and the demand they add is publication: the safety records of the plants had never been shown to the people who worked inside them. Article 35(1) now requires the condition of every installation to be measured continuously and published.
1974-76
The Assembly Reform. The Directorate is dissolved. Planning is rebuilt as rounds of negotiation between workplace, communal and regional councils, with everything published. The week is cut to thirty-two hours. This reform, not the 1890 revolution, is where most of Title IV of the present Constitution actually comes from.

Opening

1978
Environment and Justice enters government for the first time. The first ecological accounts are published, in tonnes and hectares, for eleven industries.
1983
The Vandorum replanting begins, on a hundred-year schedule. It is not finished.
1985
The Republic begins exporting uranium concentrate, and later fuel assemblies and specialised components. Article 270(2) prohibits any nuclear weapon absolutely and always has; every export licence since the first has had a safeguards condition and a prohibition on transfer to a weapons programme.
1989
The Corvaluri crossing opens to Iosmein after ninety-nine years shut.
1994
The Democratic Socialist Bloc is founded at Lindoma by Fluid, Iosmein and Reformed Luces Aporia.
1998
The hills above Corcina are found to hold the best wind resource in the country and the renewable programme begins in earnest. Fission is reclassified, first by policy and in 2026 by Article 32(6), as a transitional source with a certified date of lapse.
2001
The Republic adopts free and open software across public administration, initially to escape a licensing dispute with a foreign supplier, and later on principle.
2003
The first foreign economic entities are licensed to operate inside the Republic, handling the shipping, certification, insurance and brokerage of the export trade, which the Republic's own institutions are not equipped to conduct in foreign jurisdictions. The licences are revocable, limited in time, and conditioned on the transfer of technique to the knowledge commons. Article 21 now states those conditions on the face of the Constitution.
2008-12
The financial crisis passes through Fluid mostly as collapsed orders from abroad. The Republic sends materials and medical staff to two Bloc members and receives nothing in return, as expected.
2013-19
The climate arrives on schedule. Coastal retreat at Ravelluri, three failed harvests in Quintorum, and the Sornorum floods of 2017, which displace four thousand people from a floodplain the spatial plans had never stopped anyone building on.

The Deadlock

2019
The governing coalition breaks over the pace of the ecological transition. The Humanist Socialists want the fossil phase-out slowed to protect the Vandorum and Kelvaqui workforces; the Democratic Socialists and Environment and Justice want it accelerated. Neither side can assemble a majority.
2019-23
Four years of paralysis. The 1890 text had no rule for dissolving the Council, no rule for what happens when no government can be formed, and no court with power to say so. The Council sits, cannot act, and cannot be sent home. The Presidium governs by emergency decree for forty-seven months, on an emergency power that was written for floods.
2022
The Presidium's third emergency renewal is challenged and the Supreme Court finds that it has no jurisdiction to hear the case. The judgment runs to one page.
2023
The Lindoma Assembly, a body of nine hundred residents drawn by lot and convened by the communal councils without any legal authority to do so, sits for five weeks and demands a new constitution. A referendum in October authorises drafting, with 71 per cent in favour.

The Second Constitution

2024
The second Constitution is adopted on 1 March, drafted in eight months by a committee of the Council itself. It is generous and short: a rights catalogue, cooperative ownership of the means of production, the abolition of currency, and two safeguards written in the fear that the deadlock had been engineered from outside. It has seven articles and no executive branch.

The Long Winter

Sep 2024
The first failure. The Electoral Commission classifies two parties under Article 2 and the State of Democratic Safeguard is declared, suspending ordinary legislation. One of the two is the National Traditionalists, at four per cent. The other is a housing campaign formed that spring and polling at nineteen. Two hundred thousand people are on the streets of Lindoma within a fortnight, most of whom despise both parties. The Safeguard is lifted in November and the power is never used again.
2024-25
The second failure. Currency is abolished in Article 3 and no procedure replaces it. Through the winter, insulation, timber, antibiotics and spectacle lenses run short in particular places, and there is no lawful way to rank claims, ration, or queue. Allocation happens anyway, informally, through acquaintance and position. The barter that the text prohibits becomes the way things are obtained.
10 Jun 2025
The third failure. Turnout at the ordinary election is 57.3 per cent, below the sixty per cent that Article 2 makes a condition of validity. The election is void. The text says nothing whatever about what happens next.
Jun-Sep 2025
The Vacancy. For fourteen weeks the Republic has no parliament and no lawful means of getting one. Water, power, food, clinics and schools keep running, administered by communal councils and branch federations acting on their own authority. This is remembered, and it is the reason general competence now sits with the commune under Article 167.

The Third Constitution

Sep 2025
A referendum convenes a Constitutional Convention: 200 members, elected in the twenty sub-regions by open list, sitting in public, answerable to bodies drawn by lot.
Nov 2025 - Feb 2026
Drafting. Deliberative bodies drawn by lot examine each title and publish their objections. Reasoning systems built to the Convention's own specification and held in common are used to check cross-references, model resource consequences, and find the contradictions a human reader misses at this length. Every choice they informed was made and recorded by a person, which Article 339 now states on the face of the text.
12 Apr 2026
The Convention adopts the draft by 148 votes to 52.
7 Jun 2026
Referendum. Approved by 64.1 per cent of votes cast. No turnout condition applies, and none ever will again.
7 Jul 2026
Commencement. The constitutions of 2024 and 1890 are repealed and preserved. The Judicial Council is to be drawn within a hundred and twenty days, the first Assembly of two hundred elected within a year, and the first ecological budgets published within two.

9Assessment (Opinion)

Why the first constitution lasted

Eleven articles governed for a hundred and thirty-four years, which is a long time for a document that says almost nothing. It lasted because it was mostly procedural and because the practice grew up around it: consultation after the Hungry Springs, rationing by commune in two blockades, negotiated planning after the strike wave. None of that was written down. When the coalition finally broke in 2019, the Republic discovered that a constitution of conventions holds only for as long as its parties consent to it.

What the Directorate taught

The Directorate was not a coup and no one defended it as an ideal. It grew because an embargo made secrecy defensible, and then the secrecy outlived the embargo. It failed in a specific and instructive way: targets in tonnage produced heavy goods and killed repair, and an unpublished plan could not be argued with by the people it governed. Both lessons are now constitutional. Article 90(4) publishes the plan and its variants. Article 33 requires goods to be repairable by their user. Article 116 requires arduous tasks to be registered publicly and engineered away, which is what the Lutoliluri dockers were asking for in 1971. The reactors are the sharpest case of all: a programme that kept a blockaded country in electricity was run for sixteen years without publishing a single safety record, and the people most exposed to it were the last to be told anything.

Why the second constitution failed

The 2024 text was written by people who had watched four years of paralysis and drew the wrong lesson from it. They concluded that the danger was hostile ideology, and armed the state against it with a classification power and a turnout threshold. Both weapons went off in the Republic's own hand: the classification power was used against a housing campaign, and the turnout threshold converted a bad day into a constitutional vacuum. Meanwhile the text abolished currency and forgot to say how anything would be allocated instead, which produced the one thing an eco-socialist republic can least afford, a black market in necessities.

What the third constitution is for

It is longer than both of its predecessors combined. The 1890 text was silent and relied on convention. The 2024 text was aspirational and relied on good faith. This one writes down what happens when things go wrong: when an enterprise fails, when a commune defaults, when an election cannot be held, when the plan runs short, when a majority wants to do something the Republic has decided no majority may do. Article 319 concedes the cost of that, and Article 318 makes the Republic review that for it every ten years.
