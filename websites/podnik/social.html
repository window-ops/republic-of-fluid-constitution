Commitments, evidence and design advice

The room inside the vehicle

A tram is somewhere people spend twenty minutes of their day in the company of their neighbours. The federation has committed to making that time worth something, has measured what its seating arrangements actually do, and publishes the result whichever way it came out.

The commitment

What the federation has promised

Loneliness has been rising among the youngest residents for about fifteen years. The user assemblies raised it first, the sub-regional councils took it up, and the federation assembly accepted a share of the work in 2021. The causes are many and they are specific to this country. Explanations borrowed from capitalist societies, where people are cut off by insecure work, housing nobody can afford and the loss of public space, describe conditions the Republic does not have.

The federation holds one significant function. Podnik decides what the inside of a vehicle looks like, how long a person waits at a stop, and whether a journey can be shared.

The promise is written into the rules. The factories design for company, the operating cooperatives keep waiting times inside the range below, and the federation measures the effect and publishes it. The 2021 resolution obliges the assembly to check the promise every three years against evidence.

Reliability

Waiting time is the first commitment

The size of the fleet exists to hold this range.

  • Ten minutes is the working range

    A gap of up to ten minutes on a town service passes without comment and costs nobody an arrangement. The operating cooperatives are held to that figure by their user assemblies, and the position feed publishes every departure transparently.

  • Exactness is a smaller matter

    A vehicle running three minutes off its time troubles nobody here. The federation has declined the second-by-second standard that some networks abroad hold to, on the view that it buys precision at a cost in staff pressure the cooperatives are unwilling to impose.

  • Outages are the real harm

    An hour with nothing running takes the evening away from everyone who planned around it, and a route that does that twice in a month loses riders who then stop arranging to meet at all. The reserve fleet exists to prevent that failure.

  • People plan around the network

    People arrange to meet because they expect a vehicle to come. Every hour of unexplained absence undermines a little of that assumption, and it is slow and expensive to rebuild.

Two words

Sociopetal and sociofugal

The terms are borrowed and the federation uses them in their ordinary sense.

  • Sociopetal

    Arrangements that draw people together

    Facing pairs, four-seat groupings, side benches facing each other across the aisle, and the open standing zone where people end up in a loose circle. A person seated this way can hold a conversation without turning their body.

  • Sociofugal

    Arrangements that hold people apart

    Forward-facing rows, seats backing on to seats, single seats against a window, and standing space facing a screen or a door. A person seated this way can travel a whole line without meeting anyone's eye.

  • Both belong in a vehicle

    Wanting to be left alone on a journey is fair, and a rider who has spent a shift among people has a right to a seat where nobody looks at them. A design offering only one of the two fails somebody every trip.

  • Capacity pulls one way

    Sociofugal layouts hold more people in the same floor area, which is why the metro cars and the busiest articulated buses lean that way. The federation accepts this on the lines where the passenger numbers require it.

The experiment

How the survey was run

  1. Two groups, defined by where people sat

    The control group was riders sitting in sociofugal spaces, together with riders boarding models laid out sociofugally throughout. The test group was riders in sociopetal spaces and models.

  2. Fourteen sub-regions, twelve months

    March 2024 to February 2025, across trams, town buses, coaches and regional trains. 9,640 residents answered at least once, 4,780 in the control group and 4,860 in the test group.

  3. Asked at the end of the journey, and again later

    The first questions were put as riders left the vehicle. Everyone reporting any contact was asked again at six months to find out whether anything had lasted.

  4. Published whole

    The questions, the raw returns with names removed, and the working of every figure below are published with the annual report. Anyone may put a question about the method and receive a reasoned answer.

The last two rows are the cost of the first five. About one rider in five in a sociopetal space disliked being in view, and one in nine moved away from it.

Reading it honestly

What the survey does not show

The effect on lasting connection is small. A rise from 1.2 per cent to 2.1 per cent means that roughly nine journeys in a thousand ended in something still standing half a year later.

People choose their seats. Riders who want company are more likely to sit in a sociopetal space, so part of the difference between the groups reflects who sat where. The model-level comparison, which removes the choice by comparing whole vehicles, gives a smaller gap on every measure.

The discomfort is real and some people feel it far more. The riders who reported feeling watched were mostly young women, people travelling alone late in the evening, and riders who had said they have a disability. The federation treats a layout that makes those groups uncomfortable as a fault in the layout.

Nothing here was measured against the causes. The survey compares two seating arrangements. It says nothing about why loneliness among the youngest has risen, and the federation has neither the skill nor the standing to look into that.

Design advice

Guidance to the factories

This is advice only. The factories design their own vehicles and the communes adopt their own fleets.

  1. Balance the two, and let neither take over

    On a town vehicle, hold sociopetal seating between 40 and 60 per cent of the seated capacity. A cabin given wholly to facing seats is too much for the riders who wanted a quiet trip, and a cabin of forward-facing rows offers nobody anything.

  2. Keep the other kind within reach

    Every seat should sit within six metres of a seat of the opposite arrangement, so that a rider who finds themselves uncomfortable can move around without leaving the vehicle.

  3. Break the run

    No more than three facing bays in an unbroken row. A block of sociofugal seating or a standing bay between them gives the eye somewhere to rest and stops the cabin looking like a waiting room.

  4. Leave room between knees

    1,450 mm between the backs of facing seats and 720 mm of clear knee space. Contact that a rider has not chosen is the most common complaint about facing pairs, and the dimension fixes most of it.

  5. Keep seats for being alone

    There should be at least four single seats per vehicle with nothing facing them and a window alongside.

  6. Doors and high loadings stay sociofugal

    Door areas, the space over the wheel arches and the whole layout of the high-capacity metro cars are better left to encourage flow and maximize standing room. Talking at a door helps nobody who is trying to get off.

Proposed

Portable bookshelves in trams

The Cortius user assembly has proposed a trial. Small movable bookcases would be fixed at the joint in the middle of the FER T4 and T5, holding perhaps forty volumes, with books free to take and free to leave. The proposal came from riders and reached the federation assembly in May 2026.

The engineering questions are settled. Ferrista has already created a bracket that mounts on the existing floor bolts and comes out in ten minutes for cleaning. Loaded mass is under 30 kg, which changes no axle load in the catalogue. The case sits clear of the aisle width the test station at Vestrano requires.

The open questions remain. Who restocks it, what happens when it is emptied in a week, and whether a shelf of books earns its floor area on a line where people stand. The assembly decides in November 2026, and a trial would run on three Cortius lines for a year with the same reporting as the seating survey.

None of this is claimed as a solution. The federation runs services and decides on the designs. It has committed to doing both in a way that gives people a reasonable chance of company, and to measuring whether that works. The measures are partial, the effects are small, and the causes lie mostly elsewhere.
