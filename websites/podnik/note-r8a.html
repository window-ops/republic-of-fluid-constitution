Design note · Southern operating cooperative with the Sederna Carriage Factory

How the R8/A was worked out

A relief driver kept a clock in his pocket and reported himself for half a second. This note gives the drawings, the mass ladder and the braking work that answer him, set against the Japanese commuter practice he was trained in. It is a working paper of the drafting group and states no decision of the federation.

Drawing 1

The unit against the reference, to scale

The R8/A keeps the 56.20 m of the R8 over three cars and three door pairs a side. The Japanese reference is a three-car formation of 20 m cars with four door pairs a side, which is the arrangement the driver worked to in Osaka.

Drawing 2

End elevation and what sits under it

The body is narrowed by 150 millimetres so the unit stays inside the gauge on the 110 m curves of the southern route at the approach speeds this proposal implies. The reference runs on 1,067 mm track, which we do not have.

Drawing 3

The mass ladder

Twelve tonnes come out of the shell. An aluminium extrusion carries the load at 84.0 tonnes empty against 96.0 tonnes for the steel R8, and the loaded axle falls from 21.6 to 18.4 tonnes. The reference sits heavier as a formation and far lighter on the axle, because it spreads 99 tonnes over twelve axles on lighter track.

Drawing 4

The last two hundred metres

It is where most of the delay can be reduced. The R8 brakes softly and blends the regeneration, so the driver commits early and corrects by feel. The R8/A computes the curve for the measured load and the measured rail, follows it, and releases into the target stop. The reference is drawn at the deceleration a pattern-controlled commuter set works to.

  • SED R8 in service, 0.90 m/s²
  • SED R8/A corrected, 1.10 m/s²
  • Commuter reference, 1.25 m/s²

Drawing 5

What the pocket clock recorded

The driver logged twenty-four stops on the southern route through one week in June, measured from the front of the cab to the end of the platform less one metre, which is the rule he set himself. The band is the tolerance specified for the R8/A, and it is the figure Japanese automatic stopping equipment is tuned to.

The cost of the aluminium

What the drafting group argued about

The build account rises and the running account falls. An aluminium shell costs 18 tonnes of CO2e more to make than the steel one it replaces, and returns it through 2 grams a vehicle-kilometre over a forty year life. The group records that a vehicle bought for punctuality alone would fail the test, and that this one passes it on the energy.

Repair had to survive the change. A welded extrusion is harder to open than a bolted steel shell, so the sections are drawn to be cut and replaced whole at the half-life rebuild, and the factory holds the jigs. Nothing in the design leaves a depot mechanic unable to work on it.

The correction does not directly assist the driver. The system follows the computed curve over the last two hundred metres and releases at any touch of the brake handle instead of keeping it. The group considered a full automatic stop of the Japanese kind and rejected it, since it needs marked platforms, gates and a signalling system that regulates a train to the second, none of which exists here and none of which is proposed.

Fifteen centimetres of width cost seats. The narrowed body takes the seated count from 138 to 132 and the total from 306 to 292. On a route running eight-minute headways at the peak the group accepted the loss. On a busier route it would refuse it, and the R8 remains the standard vehicle everywhere else.

Drawing 6

The platform, and where trains actually stop

A southern route platform is 70 m of unpainted concrete with a name board, a shelter and no gates. The target stop puts the front of the cab one metre inside the far end, which brings all three cars alongside and every door onto the surface. The lower panel repeats that point at an expanded scale, with the same twenty-four recorded stops and the tolerance the R8/A is specified to meet.

Drawing 7

How the correction works

Everything in the loop is measurement the train already takes. The line record is the published survey of gradient and curve for the route, held as a file in the register. The load comes off the air suspension pressure, which is how the brake already knows the weight of the car. Adhesion is estimated from the slip the wheels report under the first application. The solver returns one braking curve and the blend follows it.

Drawing 8

The four states, and the way out of all of them

The system arms itself when the line record says a stop is 400 m ahead and the driver has made no application. It follows the curve, releases into the target stop and reports the error it finished with. A touch of the handle at any point drops it to standby for the rest of the approach, and it stays there until the train has left the platform.

Drawing 9

Where the uncertainty goes

Position error is largest far out and collapses as the train closes on the mark, because the wheel count has less distance left in which to drift and the slip is measured rather than assumed. The line record is the one term that stays flat, since it is the resolution the survey was taken at. Reaching below it would mean surveying the route again at a finer step.

Drawing 10

A wet patch at 120 metres

This is one recorded approach on the test track with water laid down deliberately, taken as the worst case for the specification. The demand stays flat while the measured deceleration falls under the wet. The lower panel gives what that costs in position, in centimetres, on a scale of its own. The solver recovers it over the remaining hundred metres by asking for slightly more than the curve requires, and the error is zero at the target stop.

Drawing 11

What the rail does to the stop

Braking distance from 70 km/h under the three conditions the southern route actually offers. The R8 loses most in leaf fall because the driver has to commit early and cannot know what the rail will be like. The corrected unit measures the slip and pays for the wet in distance rather than in accuracy, which is the whole trade.

Nothing here is proprietary

The correction is published, and so is the route it reads

A vehicle that stops itself is a vehicle that decides something about a passenger, so the deciding part is public. The solver, the estimator and the line record format are held in the federation repository, the survey of every route is a file anybody may download from vcs.podnik.rof/rolling/surveys, and a depot that changes anything writes the change into its own register. A cooperative may run the unit with the correction switched out entirely.

git clone https://vcs.podnik.rof/rolling/r8a-approach
cd r8a-approach
./configure --with-line-record --without-telemetry
make && make check
make record ROUTE=southern    # rebuilds the survey file from the register

Drawing 12

Where the work stands

The group has the shell and the estimator. The braking work is done on the test track and undone on the route, which is the part that takes a year. Nothing is promised for a date, and the entry in the catalogue counts one unit because one unit is what exists.

Standing of this note. A working paper of the drafting group, published because everything is published while it is being drafted. The proposal itself sits with the assembly of the federation. Nothing here binds a cooperative, a commune or a factory, and the figures for the reference vehicle are taken from published sources abroad.
