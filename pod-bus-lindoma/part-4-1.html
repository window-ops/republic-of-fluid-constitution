An empty car§

The 28 runs inland from the harbour. It's a little after five, and the four notes for Calmura play inside a car where most of the rows are empty and the sun comes through the windows in long bars. Vlad sits near the middle door with his bag on his knees. Two seats along, a woman in a grey jacket has spent three stops looking at the empty rows, then at him, then down at her hands, gathering herself.

Akima

turning only her head, her voice low Excuse me, is something wrong in the city today? The tram is empty.

Vlad

No, there are many trams that run empty all around the city until they collect people. Like, there always has to be one coming.

Akima

At home a car this empty would be taken off the timetable by spring. a small pause It still comes every eight minutes?

Vlad

Usually it does, although sometimes it comes double that.

Akima

looking up at the bare panel above the door, where a screen would be Sixteen minutes. quietly And nothing above the door tells you which stop follows. How do people learn the order?

Vlad

You remember the stops, and then there is OpenStreetMap.

Akima

lifting her phone a little, then setting it back on her knee I have it downloaded. I still count the stops in my head. the four notes for Serpina play And the melody helps, though it took me a year to hear it as one thing.

Departure songs§

Vlad

They have been inspired from Tozai. This is the only line in Lindoma that has departure songs.

Akima

a small nod Baraki-Nakayama was my station, two before the end. Every line at home has them, four or five seconds each, all separate.

Vlad

It's in Tokyo, right?

Akima

Chiba. a small correction in her voice Funabashi, at the eastern end. The line goes into Tokyo.

Fourteen months§

Vlad

You seem new around here.

Akima

straightening slightly Fourteen months. hesitating, then Is it obvious?

Vlad

Yeah, but it doesn't bother me. Like, I am from Romania.

Akima

turning a little more toward him Romania. seeming to weigh whether the next question is permitted Did it take you long to stop apologising for things nobody minded?

Vlad

No, I usually don't apologize for things that nobody minds except when I am very anxious.

Akima

a short, quiet breath close to a laugh Then you are ahead of me. I apologise for asking the tram question.

Vlad

You don't have to, it was a good question.

Akima

looking at the empty seats again At home an empty car in the afternoon would mean a fault on the line. Here it means the city has enough trams. quietly I keep reading the wrong meaning into things.

Vlad

Why would it mean a fault on the line?

Akima

Because the cars are always full, so an empty one means something ahead has stopped and the crowd is being held at the last station. turning her hands over on her knees You see it before any announcement comes.

The seats and the space§

Vlad

No transport here is ever full, there is always some space. pointing to the seats And they designed those things thinking that you would always have a place to move.

Akima

following where he points, at the wide gaps between the rows I noticed it on the first day and could not name what was strange. a pause There is room to stand without touching anyone.

Vlad

There are the seats on the back for those that do not want to talk, so you could have chosen those.

Akima

glancing back at the rear rows, then away I read the notice at the depot in my second week. Before that I thought every seat meant leave me alone, because at home that is what all of them mean.

Vlad

If you imported any metro from Tokyo in here, the seats would lose their meaning because although they say it's sociofugal, meaning leave me alone, they are not quite like that because they are so clustered.

Akima

considering that You are right. A bench along the wall pressed shoulder to shoulder is nobody's private seat. looking at the facing pairs across the aisle We were taught not to look at each other, and that was the only wall we had.

Sitting closer§

Vlad

And right now you are looking at me, why not sit closer?

Akima

not moving for a moment, then gathering her bag and taking the seat across from him Like this?

Vlad

This could work. Usually for clarity you sit near the person but if you want space you can sit on the other side, although in crowded environment, you would want the person you are talking to be near you.

Akima

settling her bag on the floor beside her feet The woman I go to would say the same. She calls it seating by practicality. a small pause She also told me that silence is never owed to anyone here.

No fare gates§

Vlad

In general, we trust each other.

Akima

nodding slowly I understood that from the fare gates. There are none. looking toward the front of the car where a driver would sit At home the trust is in the system working while here it seems to be in the people.

What made her come§

Vlad

Yeah, and then what made you come here?

Akima

I left the flat at ten past six and came back near eleven. The commute was ninety minutes each way, standing, and the last part of the evening was unrecorded, so on paper I worked eight hours.

Vlad

Well, here, everything is recorded, and if there are no traces, then of course you can ask people to help and say what they saw, go to court, say your constitutional rights have been violated, and then an investigation will begin and it is almost certain that the will be in your favor.

Article 72 and the four day week§

Akima

reciting it the way it was taught to her Article 72. Twenty four hours over four days, and disconnection outside working time. They gave us the article on the first morning and I thought it was a welcome speech. looking at her hands It is enforceable.

Vlad

Of course it is. There is this principle, that freedom begins where work ends.

Akima

quietly, testing the sentence Freedom begins where work ends. In Japanese it would sound like a slogan on a poster nobody reads.

Vlad

How is it pronounced in Japanese?

Akima

saying it slowly for him Jiyuu wa shigoto ga owaru tokoro kara hajimaru. a small shrug Long. It fits better on a wall than in a mouth.

Vlad

What does each word mean if it fits on a wall and not a mouth?

Akima

counting them off on her fingers Jiyuu is freedom, shigoto is work, owaru is to end, tokoro is place, hajimaru is to begin. Kara means from. The verb waits until the end, so you hear freedom, then work, then wait.

Vlad

pronouncing in Romanian Libertatea începe atunci când munca se termină. Still long. I use freedom as subject, begins as verb, and then I need to use the conditional and the impersonal, so when work is ended, rather than ends.

Akima

listening carefully to the shape of it Yours puts the freedom first and mine puts it last. almost smiling Sixteen syllables in mine. The Fluidian version on the depot wall is seven.

Vlad

Regardless of whether freedom stands first or last, freedom doesn't really matter for employers. Putting it last makes it more literal.

Akima

nodding at that My last year I counted around twenty five hundred hours. Someone in Romania works maybe seventeen hundred. Here it is under twelve hundred. The sentence was written where it was needed least.

Vlad

My system once had twenty five hundred logs when it booted up, but yeah... you must have been tired from working insane hours.

Akima

stops, then starts again, choosing the words with effort It is a strange thing to say out loud, because you can put my year next to a boot log and the number sounds the same. I need you to understand it was ordinary. Nobody around me was working less. There was no version of me who was tired in a way that anyone would have noticed.

Why nobody changed it§

Vlad

So you all have been collectively stuck.

Akima

sitting very still Yes. And nobody was doing it to us by then. quietly The people who could have changed it were tired in exactly the same way.

Vlad

It was an open secret.

Akima

a slow nod Everyone knew. There were laws, caps on overtime, ministry campaigns with posters. looking down We complied on paper and went home at eleven.

Vlad

I thought that kind of life is really bad and impossible to sustain healthily, especially that I think everyone has a social and hobby life in some way or another.

Ninety minutes standing§

Akima

turning her head toward the window I never slept on the train, ninety minutes standing each way. pausing I was awake the whole time and did nothing with it.

Vlad

Awake, standing, not leaning by the window, not sleeping, right?

Akima

Standing, holding the strap, no window to lean on. her hand closes slightly, remembering the shape of it You face the doors and you wait.

Vlad

Even if the trains arrive on time.

Akima

slowly shaking her head It arrived to the second and it changed nothing. quietly You are still there for ninety minutes either way.

Karoshi§

Vlad

I am trying to imagine, but it's hard, sorry if I have bad empathy.

Akima

shaking her head again You have been listening for six stops, which is more than anyone at home did. stops, then goes on anyway We had the words for it. Karoshi is in the dictionary, everyone can say it. Saying that you personally were unhappy was a part that was never meant to be said out loud.

Vlad

Karoshi... sudden death from overwork. You could have actually died, and Frostpunk allowed you to impose 14-hour and 24-hour shifts that were even deadlier.

Akima

the word lands harder coming from him A man in my section died on a Sunday, at home, thirty eight. quietly The company sent flowers and the section was back at work on Monday.

Vlad

Back to work... we are bringing the budget back to surplus next year.

Akima

catching the tone Something close to that was said, yes. looking at him for the first time You are angry on my behalf.

The wrong greeting§

Vlad

I am just trying to listen to you and understand. I don't understand your culture very well.

Akima

lowering her eyes again You do not have to understand the culture. a small pause It helps more that you asked what the words meant.

Vlad

I am Vlad by the way.

Akima

inclining her head, a half bow that she catches and does not finish Akima. offering her hand instead, a little late I still do the wrong greeting.

Vlad

shaking her hand It's okay, but yeah, the correct greeting is the universal handsake and wave.

Akima

her grip careful, then releasing The woman told me the same. drawing her hand back into her lap At home the bow tells you where you stand. Here the hand tells you nothing, which I am told is the point.

Nobody to defer to§

Vlad

Because it is, we are all supposed to be equal.

Akima

nodding At the cooperative I kept waiting to be told who to defer to. There is a coordinator and she is elected and she washes her own cup.

Vlad

Because she is not above you.

Akima

a small breath She had to tell me twice to stop standing up when she came into the room. looking at the floor The second time she sat down next to me so I could not do it again.

Vlad

Maybe she has found an intelligent way to make sure thar stuff doesn't trigger, and then a common language is touch.

Akima

her shoulder lifts slightly at the memory She put her hand here when she sat down. pausing I froze, and she left it there anyway until I stopped.

Nobody has hugged me§

Vlad

I don't know, but for me it would have have seemed to be hugged or touched, and I quickly learned with Sarena and Marliene that when you become friends with a stranger, they hug you, and Marliene became attached to me on my way back to Lindoma after my last vacation and I liked it, and the reason she was talking to strangers is because she was hoping she could befriend someone who would offer her frequent company.

Akima

listening to all of it without interrupting So they touch you before they know you well. I have been here fourteen months and nobody has hugged me, and I had assumed that was normal here.

Vlad

If you don't go to activities, talk to people, then they never hug you.

Akima

Then it was me. I go to work and I come back on this tram. a small pause I thought I was being polite.

Champagne socialism§

Vlad

We all six hours a day and have the Friday as a weekend, so we do a lot of suff, and at a block party I heard that they were cracking champaign and saying champagne socialism.

Akima

something loosening in her face I heard it from the window. My building had one in the courtyard in the spring, and somebody shouted that line and everyone laughed. quietly I watched it from upstairs and went to bed.

Vlad

So you slept.

Akima

shaking her head I lay there listening to it until it stopped. a pause It went on until two, and nobody was tired the next morning.

Vlad

Nobody was tired next morning, but you?

Akima

quietly I was fine. I had slept four hours and that was still more than at home. looking away I went to work and nobody could tell I had been awake.

Vlad

How many hours did you sleep in your flat back home?

Akima

Four and a half, sometimes five on a Saturday. Home by eleven, asleep by one, awake at half past five.

The block on the diagonal§

Vlad

I live in Marginal 20 by the way, so I recognize the courtyards.

Akima

turning to look at him again The block on the diagonal behind yours. a small pause I can see your courtyard from my kitchen window.

Vlad

So, we live pretty close, but I haven't seen you ever, not even your dark arranged hair.

Akima

touching the back of her hair without thinking I leave at seven and come back at four. And I take the stairs on the far side, so nobody has to greet me.

Vlad

So, you have been hiding all this time.

Akima

Hiding is a strong way to say it. I would have called it staying out of the way.

The list of words she keeps§

Vlad

Yeah, we're mostly strong and serious here, so...

Akima

thinking about the vocabulary itself I have been keeping a list. People here say hiding, lonely, wanting, avoiding. I would say holding back, keeping distance, being considerate.

Vlad

Well, if our history is harsh, what else to say?

Akima

Ours was harsh in the other direction. You learn the soft words because the strong ones make trouble for everyone in the room. a pause Saying I am lonely puts a duty on whoever hears it, so you say you are managing.

Vlad

Here it would be a lie, and people would say you are not fine.

Akima

the corner of her mouth moving The coordinator said exactly that to me in my second week. quietly I told her I was managing and she asked what I was managing without.

Vlad

I personally hate being told such "lies" and when I am told with such words, I often feel the relationship is declining.

Akima

I did not know it read that way. Three people at work stopped inviting me and I thought they were being considerate. They thought I did not care about them.

Saying it in full§

Vlad

You must explain to the people what words you use and tell them that you do care, and show that you do in more direct ways.

Akima

taking it in Say it directly. practising under her breath, then aloud I would like to be invited again. looking at him That is the whole sentence?

Vlad

That is the whole sentence.

Akima

Then I have been overcomplicating it. the four notes for Marquena play This is my stop.

Another loop§

Vlad

Do another loop or don't have the time?

Akima

I have nothing until Monday. the doors open and close on an empty platform I keep forgetting I have free hours.

Vlad

In every other country you get out, but here it's reliable. starting to be sarcastic And you are slacking and waiting to get topped up with a shit ton of resources.

Akima

catching the sarcasm a second late, then playing along Yes. Sitting on a tram doing nothing, at public expense. almost dry They should record it and send me a report.

Vlad

And the driver should apologize that the tram was... turning to look at the departures board sixteen seconds early.

Akima

the joke landing somewhere real There is a driver on the southern line who does that. quietly I read the interview twice and I could not tell whether I was laughing at him.

The test train§

Vlad

The cooperative thought that rather than sending complaints, they would send a draft to Podnik and manufacture the test SED R8/A.

Akima

shaking her head slowly People called it the Japanese train. The correction only engages once his hand leaves the lever, so he has to let go to get it, and he still came in a few seconds out. Better, slightly. quietly They gave it panels above the doors, proper signage, soft seats, a wide flat cab. It looks like home and it is nothing like it.

Vlad

Then why they have designed this "japanese train" anyways?

Akima

thinking it through I believe they wanted to give him something. A machine that answers faster, so the gap between his hand and the brake is smaller. a pause No urban train at home stops itself on the mark. The driver does that. They built him the thing they imagined we had.

Vlad

But in the experiment, in the stations where he was aproaching with the hand off the lever, the train was closer to being exactly on time and aligned properly.

Akima

Then the machine is better than a hand. quietly Which is the part he will find hardest, because the skill was his.

Vlad

But I still wonder whether the system is mature enough to be trusted, because it is absurd to me at least.

Akima

looking at the empty seats again Here a train can be late and nothing happens. quietly At home the whole day is stacked on that minute, so there is no room for the system to be wrong.

The slip at the gate§

Vlad

I think it's worse for everyone when the system never has to be wrong and the competition is unsustainable.

Akima

nodding slowly They hand you a slip at the gate when the train is five minutes late, so you can prove to your section that it was the railway. Everyone apologises and the day stays exactly as heavy.

Sitting apart and looking down§

Vlad

You know, you have this cold energy, even though I try to perceive you as a good person, like you look down and are seated away from me. It feels a bit aloof even though I am enjoying this conversation.

Akima

lifting her eyes to his and keeping them there I am sorry. Looking away is how I show I am listening. moving along the seat until she is straightly across from him Is this better, or should I sit beside you?

Vlad

If you are comfortable, then yes.

Akima

getting up and sitting down beside him Like this. her hands settle on her knees again I am not uncomfortable. I am only aware of it.

School§

Vlad

It's better. So, what was school like?

Akima

thinking back Long. We cleaned the classrooms ourselves, then club until six, then cram school after that. quietly By fifteen I was already keeping the hours I would keep for the rest of my life.

Vlad

So, you got to work pretty young... and some teachers back in Romania wished we could have cleaned the classrooms ourselves and even here in this republic the children clean most of the classroom themselves.

Akima

something surprised in her face The cleaning I would keep. It was fifteen minutes and it was the only part where nobody was ranked. a pause It is the six o'clock club and the cram school after it that I would take out.

Vlad

School is work. I think it's technically labor.

Akima

Then I worked from six years old.

Talking to him is not work§

Vlad

Caring and all that, and even talking to you, is work.

Akima

shaking her head This one I would not count. I like talking to you. You live on the other side of the courtyard and I have wanted to know somebody there since I arrived.

Vlad

Why back home talking to strangers was an incovenience but here you like it?

Akima

You are not quite a stranger. I have seen you crossing the courtyard, and you never saw me. after a short pause At home a stranger on a train is somebody whose evening you are taking.

A single person to call§

Vlad

My experience back home was unlike what you described, because the austerity measures only started to arrive. My mother's salary as a public worker was cut and she works eight hours a day but has to answer to phone calls even when off, although it's not that much.

Akima

It starts like that. Mine began with a few calls after hours. Then it was a report they needed by morning, then a second section to cover, then the training of the new people, and every one of them arrived as a favour I could have refused.

Vlad

Where you worked at?

Akima

A logistics company in Funabashi, on the warehouse systems side. watching the street go by I started on the scheduling desk and by the end I was the person they called when anything broke.

Vlad

There shouldn't be a single person to call when everything breaks.

Akima

turning toward him, her arm brushing his without meaning to, and pulling back a bit Sorry. quietly There should not be. There was, and I let it happen, because being the one they call was the only recognition I ever got.

The hand on the shoulder§

Vlad

It's okay, our arms sometimes brush each other.

Akima

letting her arm come back to where it was, then, slowly, deliberately, putting her hand on his shoulder the way the coordinator once put hers Like she did. leaving it there Am I doing it right?

Vlad

Yes, and why are you putting your hand on my shoulder?

Akima

keeping the hand where it is Because it worked on me. You told me something true about your mother and I had no words that were strong enough, so I am using yours.

Vlad

Every significant stranger touched my shoulder, but I didn't expect you to do the same.

Akima

holding it there a moment longer, then letting it fall to the seat between them I am trying not to be the cold one. At home I was the warm one. Everybody was further away than this.

How people here touch§

Vlad

Here we happened to be close. Perhaps many of our expressions have been physical.

Akima

watching his face rather than the window I noticed it in the first week and did not understand what I was seeing. People stand close, they touch an arm to interrupt you, they lean in over a table. I read it as pressure but it was only warmth.

Vlad

And now you are starting to do the same, looking at my face.

Akima

staying with it, though her eyes want to drop Yes. I would like to keep practising, if you will let me sit here again.

Vlad

I will. And it was good to get to know you.

Akima

her shoulders coming down slightly Then I will come down into your courtyard sometimes. Not to knock on anything, just to be in it, where people can see me.

The Tozai line, Baraki-Nakayama, Funabashi, Chiba and karoshi are real.

Akima describes Japanese work from fourteen months outside it and from the job she actually held, ninety minutes standing each way on one wage. What she says holds for that office and that line.

Akima generalises from a workplace that was hard on her and a commute that took three hours out of her day, fourteen months ago, with nobody around her to check the picture against. The offices where none of this holds were outside what she saw, and the part goes nowhere near them.
